# 11 — Generating Ideas for Any User

The same methods as [10-GENERATING-IDEAS-FOR-END-USERS.md](10-GENERATING-IDEAS-FOR-END-USERS.md) work for any product. Only the places you look change.

---

## 1. Watch people work

- **Watch real people doing the task,** not using your product. Spend 30 minutes watching a nurse, shop owner, student or accountant do their normal job.
- **Look for the signs of friction:** copy and paste between apps, sticky notes, screenshots sent to themselves, "let me just check something", and doing the same thing twice.
- **Ask about the last time,** not about wants. "Walk me through the last time you did X" beats "What would you like?" People are good at describing what happened and bad at predicting what they'd use.

---

## 2. Find workarounds

A workaround is the strongest signal there is, because someone already wants it enough to put up with a bad solution:

- spreadsheets running a business process
- WhatsApp or email used as a database or approval system
- paper forms next to a computer
- one person who does a task "because only they know how"
- browser bookmarks, templates and text snippets people built themselves

---

## 3. Read what's already written down

| Source | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Support tickets and emails | The same question asked many times |
| App store and product reviews, including competitors' | One- and three-star reviews that name a missing feature |
| Reddit, forums, Facebook groups, Discord | "Is there a tool that...", "How do you all handle..." |
| Search data | What people search for and don't find |
| Your product's analytics | Where people drop off, pages they revisit, features nobody uses |
| Job posts | Repetitive tasks companies hire people to do by hand |

---

## 4. Look at your own frustrations

- Write down every annoyance in your day for a week. Anything that annoys you many times a week probably annoys others too.
- You know your own field better than outsiders do. Problems in a niche you know well are easier to judge and easier to sell into.

---

## 5. Watch for shifts

New ideas often come from change:

- **New technology:** AI makes tasks possible that weren't before, such as reading documents, summarising calls or drafting replies.
- **New rules:** tax changes, compliance requirements and e-invoicing create work that everyone has to do.
- **New behaviour:** remote work, mobile-first users, payments moving to new apps.

Ask: "What's painful today that just became cheap to fix?"

---

## 6. Use Claude to widen the list, not to decide

- Paste in real material (reviews, tickets, forum threads, interview notes) and ask: "Group these into themes, ranked by how often they appear and how painful they sound."
- Ask Claude to walk through a day as a specific person, such as a clinic receptionist or a freelance designer, and list every point of friction.
- Ask: "What do people in this role currently do by hand that software could do?"

Without real input, Claude gives you generic ideas. With real input, it's very good at finding patterns.

---

## 7. Filter before you build

Score each idea from 1 to 5:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| How often does the pain happen? | Daily beats yearly |
| How many people have it? | Market size |
| How painful is it now? | Costs money or time, or is only annoying? |
| Are they already paying for or working around it? | Proof they care |
| Can you build a small version in days? | Fast feedback |
| Can you reach these people? | An idea you can't sell to doesn't help |

Then test cheaply before building:

- **Talk to 5–10 people** who have the problem. If none of them is excited, move on.
- **Make a landing page or a mock-up** and see if people sign up or ask for it.
- **Do it by hand first.** Do the work manually for a few users, and automate it only once they keep coming back.

---

## Summary

The same rule applies everywhere: ideas come from real friction, the best proof is an existing workaround, and the only real test is whether people use it.
